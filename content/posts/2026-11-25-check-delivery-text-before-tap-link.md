# Check a Delivery Text Before You Tap the Link

A delivery text usually arrives while you are waiting for something you actually ordered. That timing is the point. The message is short, the link is one tap away, and ignoring a real notice feels riskier than checking it, so the reasonable move becomes the dangerous one. Couriers do send notices, which is exactly why a fake one fits so easily into an ordinary day.

The good news is that this is one of the few online problems you can handle with a fixed routine instead of judgment in the moment. Here is the routine worth keeping.

## Start from what a real notice contains

A genuine delivery notice tells you something you can verify from the other direction: a carrier name, a tracking number, an order you recognize, and a delivery window or a pickup point. When one of those is missing, the message is already weak. When all of them are missing and the only content is a link and a deadline, it is not a notice. It is an advertisement for a page that wants your information.

Read the sender before the link. A delivery notice from a courier usually comes from a number the same courier uses for other messages, and it does not change every week. A string of digits you have never seen, sent at an odd hour, is a reason to slow down rather than a reason to hurry. Sender numbers can be imitated, so this is a hint, not proof, but it costs nothing to notice.

## The four details that should stop you

First, the link itself. A courier's own site has a domain you can read, and a shortened link hides where it goes. Long-press the link or copy it into a note and look at the address before opening it. Misspellings, extra words, and unfamiliar endings are the usual tells. When the link opens a page that asks you to log in with a shopping account or a bank account, close it. A courier does not need your password to hand over a box.

Second, a request for payment. Redelivery fees exist, but they are normally collected by the seller or the carrier through a channel you already use, not through a link that appears in a text at ten at night. Treat any small payment request as a full stop and check the order in the app where you bought the item.

Third, a request to install something. An app offered outside the official store, or a "security profile" that has to be added to the phone before the page will continue, is not part of any delivery. On an iPhone, a configuration profile installs quietly and can change how the phone behaves. Nothing about receiving a parcel requires one.

Fourth, time pressure built into the message: a two-hour window, a claim that the parcel will be returned tomorrow, a warning that the address has been flagged. Urgency exists to stop you from doing the check described above, so it is the clearest signal to do exactly that.

## Check from the other side instead

The reliable habit is to stop using the message as the entry point. Open the shopping app or the carrier's site yourself, type the tracking number into the box, and see whether the parcel exists. If it is there, you have your delivery window and you can ignore the text entirely. If it is not, the text was noise, and you have lost nothing but a minute.

Keep the number in one place. A note on the phone or a folder in the mail app holding the tracking numbers of everything currently in transit turns a suspicious message into a two-second lookup. It also helps on the day a parcel actually goes missing, when you need the number and the order in front of you.

## If you already tapped

Do not enter anything on the page, even to "verify" an address or cancel a request. Close the tab, and if the page keeps reappearing, clear the browser's recent tabs rather than working through its buttons.

If card details went in, call the card company's number from the back of the card rather than any number shown on the page, and ask for the card to be blocked and replaced. If a password went in, change it on the real site, and if you use that password anywhere else, change it there too. A short, calm call made today is worth more than a week of watching a statement later.

## Make it a household rule

The most useful step is not technical. Agree with the people you live with that nobody pays a redelivery fee through a link, and that any delivery message gets checked in the shopping app first. One rule, said out loud, covers the moment when someone is tired, holding a phone, and in a hurry, which is the moment these messages are designed for.

None of this requires a security app or a paid service. It requires deciding, once, that the message does not get to set the order of operations. The parcel will still arrive, the notice will still be there in the app, and the link can stay untouched.
