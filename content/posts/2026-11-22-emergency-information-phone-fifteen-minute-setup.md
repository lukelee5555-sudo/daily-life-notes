Most of us carry a small computer that knows our blood type, our medications, our spouse's phone number, and the fastest route to a hospital. Almost none of that information is set up to be useful at the one moment it matters: when we cannot unlock the screen, or when someone else is looking at it on our behalf.

Emergency information on a phone is boring to configure and easy to postpone. It takes about fifteen minutes, costs nothing, and is the kind of thing you will either be very glad you did or never notice at all. Here is a practical way to do it in one sitting, on either an iPhone or an Android phone, without turning your phone into a public notice board.

## Start with the lock screen

Both major phone platforms let you show a limited set of details from a locked screen without giving anyone access to the rest of the device. On an iPhone this is the Medical ID, reachable from the Emergency screen on the passcode pad. On Android the feature is usually called Emergency information and sits in the Safety or Emergency settings area.

Fill in the parts an emergency responder would actually act on: allergies, current medications, and any condition that changes treatment, such as a heart condition, diabetes, or a bleeding disorder. Add one or two emergency contacts. Do not list your life story. A paramedic reads this in seconds, so clear lines beat completeness.

Then set the sharing option so the information is visible while the phone is locked. You can also choose whether your emergency contacts are called from the lock screen; that is a separate switch from the health details, and many people set one and forget the other.

## Decide who is allowed to be called

An emergency contact should be someone who will answer an unknown number, knows your medical basics, and can make decisions or reach people who can. A partner is the usual first choice. A second contact is worth adding for the case where the first person is unreachable, travelling, or in the same accident.

Tell both people that you have listed them and ask whether they are comfortable with it. This sounds like an unnecessary courtesy until you consider the alternative: someone receiving a frightening call from a hospital about a person who never mentioned being their emergency contact. One short message fixes that.

## Make offline information available

Two things commonly fail exactly when they are needed: mobile data and your memory of an address.

Download an offline map of your city, and of any region you travel to regularly. Every major map app offers this now, and it takes a minute per area. Downloading the map for your own neighbourhood feels pointless until you are the person giving directions to a driver while your battery is at four percent.

Second, put the address of your home and your workplace somewhere you can read it without unlocking the phone, or memorise it. Most people can produce their home address on demand and discover, under stress, that they cannot recall the spelling of their street for a form or a taxi.

## Keep a paper copy where you live

Phones run out of power, get locked by a fall, or end up in a bag that is not with the patient. A single sheet of paper in a wallet or taped inside a kitchen cabinet does what no battery can: it works when nothing else does.

Write down your name, date of birth, allergies, medications, and the two emergency contacts with phone numbers. Add your doctor's name and clinic if you have one. Keep it short enough that someone can read it in a panic. Review it twice a year, when you change a medication, and after any new diagnosis.

## Check the backup, not just the plan

Emergency settings are only useful if they are current. Three small habits keep them alive:

1. Look at the locked emergency screen once a month. If it takes more than a couple of taps to reach, adjust the settings now rather than later.
2. Update the medication list the same day your prescription changes. Old medication entries are worse than none, because they give confident wrong information.
3. Test one contact every few months by message, not by false alarm. A simple "you are still my emergency contact, is this number right?" is enough.

## A note on children and older family members

If you manage a phone for a child or an older relative, add their emergency information in the language of the place they live in, and make sure at least one contact speaks that language. For a relative who lives alone, a printed card near the front door helps visitors and emergency services find the household quickly.

None of this replaces medical advice or a proper emergency plan. It only removes the small, avoidable friction that makes a bad day worse: a locked screen, a dead battery, a phone number nobody can remember. Fifteen minutes now is a reasonable price for that.
