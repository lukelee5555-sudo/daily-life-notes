Most people do not get hacked because someone targeted them. They get locked out, or drained, because of one of four boring things: a password reused across sites, a second factor that does not exist, an old recovery email nobody controls anymore, and a phone full of apps holding permissions the user forgot they granted. None of those requires a skilled attacker. A leaked list from any site they signed up for years ago is enough.

The good news is that all four are fixable in one sitting, and you do not need to understand the technology to fix them. Below is the order I use, because each step makes the next one easier.

## Start with a password manager, before anything else

A password manager is the only practical way to have a different password on every site, and having different passwords is what stops one leaked list from becoming ten broken accounts. Pick one and commit to it: a built-in option from your phone or browser is fine and better than a spreadsheet.

The part people get wrong is the migration. Do not try to change every password at once. Instead:

- Set one strong master password you can actually remember, and write it down on paper kept somewhere physical rather than in a note on the phone.
- Import whatever your browser has already saved. Most managers import directly from Chrome, Safari, and Edge.
- Let the manager's own audit screen tell you which saved logins are reused, weak, or involved in a breach. Work through the reused ones only when you next visit that site — a password change at the moment you are already logging in takes seconds and does not get postponed.

Two settings are worth turning on the day you install it: autofill limited to the exact site (not "any page"), and a lock that requires your fingerprint or face each time the vault opens.

## Add a second factor, but pick the right kind

A second factor means a code or approval in addition to the password. There are three common forms, and they are not equal.

- **SMS codes** are the weakest but still worth having where nothing else is offered. The main weakness is that a code sent by text can be intercepted by someone who has taken over your phone number, so use them as a fallback, not a first choice.
- **Authenticator apps** generate a code on the device itself and work offline. This is the sensible default for email, banking, and social accounts.
- **Passkeys and hardware keys** are the strongest, and passkeys are now supported by the major platforms. If a service offers "sign in with a passkey," take it; the device does the verification and there is no code to type or read out.

Whatever you use, print or save the recovery codes the service shows exactly once, at setup. Store them where you keep documents, not in a screenshot on the same phone you use to log in.

## Check where your account can be recovered

Account recovery is the quiet back door. If a password reset for your main email goes to an address you stopped using in 2019, then whoever controls that old address controls your account.

Go through your primary email, your bank, and your phone's account, and confirm three things: the recovery address is one you still read, the recovery phone number is current, and no unknown device or forwarding rule is listed. Forwarding rules are the sneakiest — a rule that copies your mail to another address keeps working long after the person who set it has your password changed.

## Review app permissions once a quarter

Every app that can read your contacts, location, microphone, photos, or notifications is a small ongoing disclosure. Most of us granted those permissions at a moment when an app asked nicely, and never looked again.

Open your phone's privacy settings, list the apps with access to location, microphone, and contacts, and remove access from anything you have not opened in the past month. The same review applies to third-party apps connected to your Google or Microsoft account: photo editors, old to-do apps, and services you tried once often retain access to your mail or files indefinitely.

## Turn on automatic updates and stop there

Updates are unglamorous and they are the single highest-value habit on this list, because most successful attacks use a flaw that the vendor has already fixed. Turn on automatic updates for your phone, your computer, your browser, and your router's firmware if it has that option. Then forget about it.

One exception: do not let a device you depend on update in the middle of something important. Schedule updates for a time you are not working rather than disabling them.

## A short list you can actually keep

- One password manager, one strong master password, written down offline.
- Every reused password changed at the next login, not all at once.
- An authenticator app or passkey on email, bank, and phone account.
- Recovery codes saved off the device.
- Recovery email and phone confirmed, and forwarding rules checked.
- App permissions reviewed quarterly, revoking anything unused.
- Automatic updates on for everything, including the router.

Total time for the first pass is usually an afternoon, and most of it is waiting for pages to load. The maintenance after that is ten minutes a quarter, which is a fair price for not spending a weekend recovering an account you cannot get back into.
