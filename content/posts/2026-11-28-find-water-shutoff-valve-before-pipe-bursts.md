Most people learn where their main water shutoff valve is on the worst possible day: standing in a hallway with water coming through the ceiling. A burst or split pipe does not wait while you search the basement with a phone flashlight. The water keeps running until someone closes the valve, and every minute it runs spreads further into drywall, insulation, and flooring.

The fix is a five-minute job you do now, in dry clothes, with the water still on.

## Find the main valve first

The main shutoff sits on the pipe that brings water into the house, usually just after the water meter. Common locations:

- A basement wall facing the street
- A utility closet, garage, or crawlspace
- Under the kitchen sink in small apartments
- Outside in a covered box near the property line, in warm climates

Look for a valve on the incoming line before any branch splits off to fixtures. If you have a meter inside, the valve is almost always within a meter or two of it.

Two valve types matter. A **ball valve** has a lever handle: a quarter turn across the pipe closes it, and the handle position tells you the state at a glance. A **gate valve** has a round wheel: turn it clockwise until it stops, which can take several full turns. Older gate valves are the ones that seize up from disuse, which is exactly why you want to find out today rather than during a leak.

## Test it while nothing is wrong

Turn the main valve off, then open a cold faucet on the lowest floor and let it run. Flow should slow and stop within a few seconds. Open a second faucet upstairs to confirm. Then turn the main back on and check that water returns at full pressure with no sputtering at the taps.

If the valve will not turn, do not force it with a pipe wrench or a hammer. A snapped stem turns a nuisance into a plumbing call with the water still on. Stop, note the location, and ask a plumber to replace the valve.

While you are at it, run the same test on the fixture shutoffs under each sink and behind each toilet. Those small valves are what let you stop a single leak without cutting water to the whole house, and they fail just as quietly as the main.

## Make it findable and reachable

A valve you cannot reach in the dark is only half useful:

- Clear the space in front of it. Boxes stacked against the basement wall are the reason people cannot get to the valve in a hurry.
- Hang a small tag or a bright zip tie on the handle, and write "MAIN WATER" on it.
- Keep a flashlight within arm's reach of the valve, not in a drawer on the other floor.
- Show everyone in the household where it is and how it closes. A teenager or a babysitter may be the one home when a supply line lets go.

## Know the street-side shutoff too

If water is pouring out and the inside valve is broken or unreachable, the utility can close the curb stop at the street. Find the cover now so you know what it looks like, but do not dig it up or operate it yourself. Call the water utility and let them do it. Cutting into a curb box or a service line is how a leak becomes an excavation.

## Frozen pipes: thaw in the right order

When a pipe freezes, the tap stops and the pipe swells. The split usually happens where you cannot see it.

1. Shut off the main valve first, so that when the ice melts the pipe is not under pressure.
2. Open the affected faucet. This gives melting water somewhere to go and tells you when flow returns.
3. Warm the pipe with a hair dryer, a heat lamp, or warm towels, starting at the faucet end and working back toward the frozen section.
4. Never use an open flame, a blowtorch, or a charcoal grill. Never leave a heater unattended against a pipe.
5. If flow does not return, or you find a split, stop and call a plumber. Water behind the ice can be a bigger problem than the ice.

Pipes freeze most often on exterior walls, in unheated crawlspaces, in garages, and where a hose is still connected to an outdoor spigot. Disconnect garden hoses before the first hard freeze, keep cabinet doors open under sinks on exterior walls during cold snaps, and keep the house warm rather than letting it drop while you are away. A slow drip from a faucet on an exterior wall costs very little water and relieves pressure while the line is at risk.

## If a pipe bursts

Shut the main valve. Open the lowest faucet to drain what is left in the lines. If water is near outlets, a panel, or an appliance, cut power to that area at the breaker only if you can reach the panel without stepping in water. Then move valuables off the floor, photograph the damage before cleaning up for the insurance claim, and call a plumber.

None of this is dramatic, and that is the point. Finding a valve, turning it once, and putting a tag on it takes less time than reading this piece. The day you need it, you will not have five minutes to spare.
