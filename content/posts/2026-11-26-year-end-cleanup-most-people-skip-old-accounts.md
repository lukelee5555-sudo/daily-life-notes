# The Year-End Cleanup Most People Skip: Old Accounts and App Permissions

December arrives with a tidy list of chores: cards to send, receipts to file, a budget to look at before the year closes. Almost nobody puts old online accounts on that list. They are invisible, they cost nothing on a given day, and they are exactly the kind of thing that turns into a problem years later — a password reset request you did not send, a charge from a service you stopped using in spring, a stranger's session still open on a device you sold.

The fix is not a big project. Two short sessions, one for accounts and one for permissions, cover most of the risk. Here is how to run them.

## Start from your own inbox

You do not need to remember which sites you signed up for. Your mail does. Search the inbox for a few ordinary words — "welcome," "verify," "receipt," "your subscription," "password reset" — and note the senders that appear. Ten minutes of this usually surfaces accounts you had forgotten entirely. Sort what you find into three piles: things you use, things you need occasionally, and things you would not miss.

The third pile is the one worth acting on. Closing an account you no longer use removes your name, address, and purchase history from a company that has no reason to keep them, and it stops marketing mail that hides real notices. Most services let you delete from the account settings page; if you cannot find it, their privacy page usually names the process, and in many countries they are required to provide one.

Keep a short record when you close something — the service, the date, and the email you used. A note on your phone is enough. If a charge appears later, you will want the date on hand.

## Cancel before you delete

Two things should happen before an account closes. First, cancel any active subscription. Deleting an account does not reliably cancel a payment already authorized on a card or an app store, so cancel the billing first and confirm it in the confirmation email. Second, export anything you want to keep: photos, documents, purchase receipts you need for taxes or warranty claims. Many services offer a download option in settings, and it often takes a day or two to arrive, so start it before you submit the deletion request.

If a service holds data you rely on but you do not want the account active, exporting first and deleting second is the safer order in every case.

## Revoke what still has access

The second session is about permissions. Every time you pressed "Sign in with Google" or "Continue with Apple" on a new site, you handed that site a durable link into your account. Old ones outlive the reason they were granted.

Google lists these under your account's security page as third-party apps with account access; Apple lists them under Sign in with Apple in your Apple Account settings. Review the list the way you would review a bank statement: anything unfamiliar gets removed, and anything you no longer use gets removed too. Removing access does not delete the account on the other side, but it does cut the connection that lets that company read your profile or send mail in your name.

Do the same for your phone. Open the app list, sort by last used, and look at what you have not touched in a year. Unused apps are not merely clutter: they hold permissions you granted once — location, contacts, microphone — and many keep background access regardless of whether you open them. Deleting an app removes the permission with it; adjusting a permission while keeping the app is the other option when you still want it occasionally.

## Clean the sessions, not just the passwords

Passwords get the attention, but open sessions are what actually let someone in. In the security settings of your main accounts — mail first, then banking, then social — there is a list of devices and sessions signed in. Sign out everything you do not recognize, and sign out old devices you no longer own. This is the single highest-value five minutes in the whole cleanup, because it closes doors that were never meant to stay open.

While you are there, turn on two-factor authentication for the mail account you use to reset everything else. If that account falls, every other account follows through password resets, so it deserves the strongest setting you can tolerate.

## The point is not minimalism

None of this requires living with fewer services or a tidier digital life. The point is that an account you forgot about is one you cannot watch, and a permission you cannot remember granting is one you cannot judge. An hour spent in December turns a pile of unknowns into a list you control — and it costs nothing to repeat next year, when the list will be shorter.
