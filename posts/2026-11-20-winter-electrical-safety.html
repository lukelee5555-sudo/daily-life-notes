Winter Electrical Safety: 12 Checks Before You Plug In a Space Heater

November 20, 2026 · Luke Lee safetyhomewinter

Every winter the same three appliances show up in fire reports: the space heater, the electric blanket, and the multi-socket extension cord that quietly became a power strip for the whole room. None of them is dangerous on its own. What goes wrong is almost always a mismatch — too much load on one outlet, too little clearance around a hot surface, or a cord that has been stepped on since last season.

Here is the check I run at the start of every cold season. It takes about fifteen minutes in total, and most items take seconds.

The outlet is the first thing to look at

A space heater rated at 1,500 watts draws close to 13 amps at a standard 120-volt outlet, or around 6.8 amps at 220 volts. In either case it is the hungriest single thing in most homes, and it should have an outlet that is doing nothing else.

  • One heater, one outlet. No daisy-chained strips, no "just a phone charger" in the second socket. A charger is fine; a second heater, a kettle, or a microwave on the same circuit is not.
  • Check the circuit's other occupants. If the lights dim or the breaker trips when the heater starts, the circuit is already loaded. Move the heater rather than the breaker.
  • Feel the plug after ten minutes. Warm is normal. Hot to the touch, a smell of hot plastic, or a discoloured faceplate means stop using that outlet and have it checked — loose contacts there are a common ignition point.

Clearance and placement

Heaters come with a stated clearance; it is usually 90 cm (3 ft) from anything combustible, and more from the front. That number assumes the appliance is working as designed, so treat it as a minimum, not a target.

  • Nothing within a metre: curtains, bedding, laundry racks, the sofa's skirt, paper.
  • Never on a carpet with a deep pile, and never on a table where a cable can pull it off.
  • Not in a doorway where someone will step over the cord, and not under a desk where a foot will find the cord instead.
  • Out of the bathroom unless it is rated for damp locations. Ordinary heaters plus splashing water is a shock risk that no thermostat prevents.

Cords and extensions

This is where most winter fires start, and it is worth being blunt: a heater should not be run from an extension cord at all. If one is unavoidable, it must be a heavy-gauge cord rated for the appliance's current, laid flat, not coiled, and not under a rug.

  • Coiled cord plus high current equals heat trapped in the coil. That is how a cord melts without ever being overloaded on paper.
  • Cords under rugs and behind furniture get crushed; crushed insulation fails silently.
  • Replace any cord with a nick, a kink that stays kinked, or a plug that no longer grips firmly. Tape is not a repair.

The three appliances people forget

Electric blankets are folded, sat on, and stored in the same bag every year. The heating wire inside work-hardens when folded in the same place repeatedly. Unfold it fully on the bed, check for thin spots by running a hand over it, and replace any blanket over about ten years old or one with a scorch mark. Never use one with a heated mattress pad on the same bed, and never tuck the edges under the mattress, which bends the wire.

Portable radiators filled with oil are safer than bare-element heaters because the surface stays below ignition temperatures, but they are heavy and get tipped. If it has no tip-over switch, it belongs in a room without children or pets, or not in the house.

Halogen and quartz heaters reach very high surface temperatures within seconds and stay hot after switching off. They need the largest clearance of all and should never be left running in an empty room.

Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms

Heating season is also the season when an alarm that has been silent for a year is discovered to be dead. Press the test button on every alarm — it takes one minute — and replace batteries on the same day each year.

If the home has a fuel-burning appliance (gas boiler, wood stove, kerosene heater) or an attached garage, a carbon monoxide alarm on each sleeping floor is not optional. Carbon monoxide has no smell, and the early symptoms — headache, drowsiness, nausea — are the same as coming down with something.

The twelve-item list

  1. Heater on its own outlet, nothing else plugged in beside it.
  2. Plug and faceplate not hot after ten minutes of use.
  3. One metre of clearance on all sides.
  4. No fabric, paper, or drying laundry within reach.
  5. Cords laid flat, not under rugs or furniture.
  6. Heavy-gauge cord only, and no coiled extension lead.
  7. Plug grips firmly; no nicks or taped repairs anywhere.
  8. Electric blanket unfolded, uncreased, under ten years old.
  9. Tip-over switch present on any floor-standing heater.
  10. Heaters switched off and unplugged when the room empties.
  11. Every smoke alarm tested, batteries dated.
  12. Carbon monoxide alarm on each sleeping level.

When to stop and call someone

Two situations are not DIY: a faceplate or outlet that has discoloured or feels hot when nothing is plugged in, and a breaker that trips repeatedly on the same circuit. Both point to wiring that is failing behind the wall, where you cannot see it. A licensed electrician is cheaper than the alternative, and no heater is worth the risk of finding out otherwise.

The rest of the list is fifteen minutes of your evening, once a year. That is a reasonable trade for a winter where the only thing that gets warm is the room.
