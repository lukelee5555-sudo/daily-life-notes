A Kitchen Fire: The First Sixty Seconds Decide It

November 27, 2026 · Luke Lee

safetyhomekitchen

A pan of oil catches fire on the stove. The flame is small at first, no bigger than a fist, and the instinct that arrives in that moment is almost always the wrong one: throw water on it, or carry the pan outside. Both make things worse. The first minute is also the minute when the fire is easiest to stop, if you already know what you are going to do.

Why water is the worst thing you can add

Cooking oil burns at several hundred degrees. Water boils at one hundred and turns into steam the instant it meets that surface, and the steam expands violently, throwing burning oil up and out of the pan. A small fire on the stove becomes a curtain of flame at head height, and the person standing over it takes the worst of it. Flour and sugar behave the same way for a different reason: a fine powder thrown into a flame can ignite as a cloud. Neither belongs near a burning pan.

Turn the heat off first

The burning oil is being fed by the burner under it, so reach for the knob before anything else. If the controls are behind the pan, reach around the side rather than over the flame, and keep your forearm away from the rising heat. Once the heat is off, most small pan fires lose their fuel supply and begin to settle by themselves. That is also why a fire in an unattended pan is more dangerous: nobody is there to turn anything off.

Cover it with something that cannot burn

A metal lid is the tool you want, and it should be within reach whenever you cook with oil. Put it on with a long oven mitt and slide it across rather than dropping it straight down, so your wrist stays out of the plume of heat. Then leave it there. Do not lift the lid to check: the fire needs no oxygen, and lifting it lets fresh air back in to oil that is still hot enough to reignite. Turn off the heat, leave the pan where it is, and let it cool for at least half an hour.

If you have no lid, a metal baking sheet or a large metal bowl can be slid over the pan. Glass and ceramic dishes are a poor choice because they can crack from the heat difference.

Do not move the pan

Carrying a burning pan to the sink or the front door spreads fire along your path, and the motion can slosh burning oil over the rim onto your hand or clothes. The sink is the worst destination in the house, because the moment the pan meets the wet basin the water flashes to steam and throws the oil back at you. Keep the fire where it is and take away what feeds it.

Oven, microwave and toaster fires

An oven fire is usually grease on the floor of the oven or on a heating element. Turn the oven off and keep the door closed; cutting off heat and oxygen usually puts it out. Do not open the door to look, because the rush of air can feed the flames and the heat that escapes is enough to burn your face. A microwave fire follows the same logic: keep the door shut and press stop. A flaming toaster should be unplugged if you can do it safely, and never tipped into the sink.

The point where you stop and leave

The pan fire is yours to handle only while it is small, contained, and you can reach the heat control. Leave the kitchen and get everyone out if the flames have reached the cabinets, the range hood or the wall, if smoke is filling the room, or if you cannot cover the pan on the first attempt. Close the door behind you to slow the smoke, get everyone outside, and call the emergency number from there. In Korea that is 119, free and answered around the clock. Never go back inside for a pan.

If you own a fire extinguisher, keep it by the exit

A small dry chemical extinguisher is worth having in a kitchen, but only if it is stored where you can reach it without walking past the fire, which usually means near the door you would leave by. Use it from a few steps back, aim at the base of the flames, and keep your back to your escape route. If the extinguisher runs out before the fire does, leave immediately. It is a tool for making an exit, not a reason to stay.

Three habits that prevent the whole problem

Turn every pan handle toward the back or the side of the stove so it cannot be knocked over. Keep a fitting lid within arm's reach of the pan you fry in, and never leave hot oil unattended, not even to answer the door. When you are finished frying, let the oil cool completely before moving the pan, because hot oil that looks calm is still hot enough to start a fire if it spills onto a burner.

None of this needs equipment or training. It needs knowing, before the smoke starts, which hand turns off the burner and which hand reaches for the lid.
